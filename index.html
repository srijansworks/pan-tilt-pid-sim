A control-theory experiment

Teaching a camera to follow a drone

I'm building a camera that sits on the ground and turns to keep my drone in the picture. Before touching real motors, I built this simulator to figure out how to make it turn smoothly. You can play with it below.

The idea

Three things to know

1

The job

A camera sits on two small motors. One turns it left and right (pan). The other tilts it up and down (tilt). Its job is to keep my drone in the middle of the picture.

2

The problem

The camera can tell how far off it is from the drone. But if it turns too slowly it falls behind. If it turns too hard it flies past the drone, swings back, and shakes.

3

The fix: PID

PID is a simple rule for how hard to turn. It looks at how far off the camera is right now (P), how long it has been off (I), and how fast the gap is changing (D).

Meet the three parts

P Push in proportion to the gap

Far from the target, push hard. Close, push gently. If P is too small, the camera crawls. If it's too big, it overshoots and shakes. In between is just right.

I Remember a gap that won't close

My motor has a dead zone: tiny commands do nothing. With a weak P, the camera stops 0.45 degrees short and just sits there. I keeps adding up that leftover gap until the motor finally moves. It closed the gap to 0.02 degrees.

D Brake before you get there

D watches how fast the gap is shrinking. If it's closing fast, D eases off so the camera doesn't blow past the target. Same P, with and without D: it swung 28% past without D, and only 4% with it.

      Why it matters

      How I'll use this

      My drone-following camera

      This is the real target. The simulator guesses a 50 millisecond delay between the camera seeing the drone and the motors reacting. My next step is to measure the real delay and the real motor speed, put those numbers in the simulator, and only then tune on the hardware. I'll start with P only, add D to stop the overshoot, and add I last.

      Other projects that chase something

      My Tello person-following project has to keep turning toward a moving person, so I expect the same trade-off there: fast but shaky, or smooth but late. Anything that chases a moving thing will run into it. Now I know what to look for when it goes wrong.

      What I want to try next

      Beating the delay by guessing where the drone will be a moment from now. And comparing this to my LunarRL project: there, a computer learned the rule by practice. Here, I tuned the rule by hand. I want to know when each one makes more sense.

      Be honest about it

      What this simulator leaves out

      • It's a simplified model for learning. It's not a copy of my real rig.
      • The motors here follow speed commands with a small lag, a speed limit, an acceleration limit, a dead zone, and a fixed delay. Real hobby servos have their own inside controller, loose gears, and limited strength.
      • The two axes don't affect each other. A real camera head has weight, balance, and cable drag.
      • The drone is a perfect dot that the camera always sees, plus a bit of random noise. There's no object detection, no lost frames, and no changing light.
      • I haven't measured my real motors yet, so settings that work here will need re-tuning on the real thing.
      • Plain PID only. No guessing where the drone is heading.
